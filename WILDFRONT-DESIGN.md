# Wildfront 0.7 implemented design

Eight participants compete in Solo, Duos or Squads across a 6,144-metre reserve. Optional practice opponents fill seats; Free Roam supports exploration. Seven storm circles alternate waiting and closing, making food, equipment and rotation routes matter.

Each player spends ten draft tokens on selection priority and starting equipment. During their public turn, they preview and confirm a point anywhere on the map. Unspent tokens add ammo. Earlier picks reserve a 60-metre buffer against enemy selections; teammates can deliberately split up or group. Obstructed placements resolve nearby, and water landings start swimming.

Forty-eight alliteratively named settlements, including fourteen dense villages with streets and twelve buildings each, 71 road links and 35 repair lay-bys connect six habitats. Ninety-one starting vehicles include eighteen two-seat motorcycles and ten motor launches. Fuel, condition, repair parts, boost and upgrades create travel decisions; sealed caches and repeating field orders reward moving into unvisited territory. Vehicle steering changes heading independently of looking around. Supply aircraft deliver contestable loot but are not pilotable.

Three gun slots retain magazine and fitted attachments when dropped. Nine discoverable mods occupy optic, barrel and handling slots; the inventory states their effects and compatibility. Players can fit, remove, replace and share them. Armor protects against bullets and animal attacks, while storm exposure and starvation bypass it. Teams have downing and interrupted revives.

Hunger limits sprinting and eventually harms players. Peaceful prey normally flees; wolves and boar are naturally hostile. Closing phases enrage and tint wildlife, making hunts more dangerous and raising restorative meat odds. Animals yield food and restorative meat, never guns, ammunition, fuel or fabricated repair parts. Sharks patrol lakes and attack swimmers while seated boat occupants remain protected. Ambient birds are visual wildlife.

The full day lasts five real minutes. Weather, moonlight, a flashlight, an emphasized storm wall/world tint and damage pulses communicate changing conditions. Offline neural-voice recordings announce shared events and player eliminations; captions identify players and optional quips add personality.

The atlas uses an actual 4096-pixel overhead render of the terrain, trees, roads, structures and water, with quieter collision-aware labels, zoom/pan, grid references, nearby transport, objectives, supplies and storm boundaries. Enemy players stay hidden after deployment. Nearby wildlife is orange and teammates are teal. The HUD keeps combat, survival, team and navigation information grouped; the equipment panel exposes gun statistics, mod slots and supply stacks.

Keyboard and mouse actions can be rebound. Opt-in proximity voice supports push-to-talk and speech-gated open mic, regardless of team, attenuated to a 75-metre cutoff. Leaving a session keeps the original terrain/foliage/atlas and resets network populations, avoiding whole-world regeneration. Nearby-object networking limits the initial animal/loot payload for joining clients.

This remains a development build. Production-quality characters, bespoke animal animations, advanced vehicle physics/navigation, broad GPU profiling, anti-cheat, internet relay/reconnect service and long human balance sessions remain future work. See GAME-GUIDE.html for exact item effects and systems.

This pass adds 57 recorded sound assets and independent sound layers, explicit hunger feedback, more wildlife with visible-animal cues, and an autumn floodplain with two additional lakes. A live, team-filtered transport feed supports the map beyond render range. Supply-crate bonus motorcycles and twelve-second survey tools create rotation and information choices; global scan warnings provide counterplay. Every village name consists of two words sharing its initial.
